1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Basic concept of force: Force is the interaction between objects.
(2) The three elements of force include: the magnitude of the force, the direction of the force, and the point of application of the force.
(3) The unit of force is the Newton, abbreviated as N, represented by the symbol N.
(4) Force is a vector.

Section 2: Common Types of Forces
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Gravity: Gravity is the force an object experiences due to the Earth's attraction.
(2) Center of Gravity: The center of gravity is the point of application of the gravitational force.
(3) Conditions for Elastic Force: There are two conditions: contact and occurrence of elastic deformation.
(4) Sliding Friction: When two objects in contact slide relative to each other, the resisting force hindering the relative sliding is called sliding friction.
(5) Gravity Formula: G = mg. Here G is the gravitational force on the object, m is the mass of the object, g is the acceleration due to gravity.
(6) Hooke's Law: Within the elastic limit, the magnitude of the spring force f and the deformation x of the spring are proportional, i.e., f = kx. Here, f is the spring force, k is the spring constant (stiffness coefficient), x is the deformation of the spring.
(7) Sliding Friction Formula: The magnitude of sliding friction f is proportional to the normal force f_N between the objects. The relationship is expressed as: f = μf_N. Here, f is the sliding friction, μ is the coefficient of kinetic friction, f_N is the normal force.
(8) When an object has a tendency for relative motion but does not actually slide, the friction produced is called static friction. The maximum value of static friction is the maximum static friction f_max, 0 ≤ f_static ≤ f_max.

Section 3: Composition and Resolution of Forces
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Composition of Forces (Resultant): In real life, objects are often acted upon by multiple forces simultaneously. We can use the equivalent method to find a single force that produces the same effect as the original several forces. This single force is called the resultant force of those forces. The process of finding this resultant force is called the composition of forces.
(2) Concurrent Forces: If several forces all act on the same point of an object, or if their lines of action intersect at a point, these forces are called concurrent forces.
(3) Resolution of Forces: In real life, we not only need to find the resultant force but sometimes also need to resolve a single force into several forces. These forces together produce the same effect as the original single force. These forces are called component forces. Resolution of forces is the inverse process of composition of forces.
(4) Parallelogram Rule: Using F1 and F2 as adjacent sides, construct a parallelogram. The direction of the diagonal represents the direction of the resultant force F, and the length of the diagonal segment represents the magnitude of the resultant force F. If the angle between the two forces is α, the relationship between the resultant F and the components F1, F2 is:
F = √(F₁² + F₂² + 2F₁F₂cosα)

III. Chapter Knowledge Summary
1. Newton's First Law
(1) Every object continues in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled to change that state by forces acting upon it.
(2) The property of an object to maintain its original state of uniform rectilinear motion or rest is called inertia, so Newton's First Law is also called the Law of Inertia.
(3) Force is the cause of changes in an object's state of motion.
(4) Force is the cause of producing acceleration in an object.
(5) Inertia is the cause of maintaining an object's motion.
(6) Mass is the measure of an object's inertia.
(7) Objects with greater mass have greater inertia; all objects have inertia.
2. Newton's Second Law
(1) The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass. The direction of acceleration is the same as that of the net force.
(2) Formula: F = ma or F_net = ma.
3. Newton's Third Law
(1) For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. The action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act along the same straight line.
(2) Formula: F = -F' (the minus sign indicates opposite direction).
Example: Pressure and support force are a pair of action-reaction forces.

Chapter 4: Momentum
I. Basic Knowledge
Section 1: Impulse and Momentum
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Impulse: The product of force and the time during which it acts is called impulse, denoted by I. It is a vector.
Formula: I = Ft
Where: I = impulse (unit N·s), F = force (unit N), t = time (unit s)
(2) Momentum: The product of an object's mass and velocity is called momentum, denoted by p. It is a vector.
Formula: p = mv
Where: p = momentum (unit kg·m/s), m = mass (unit kg), v = velocity (unit m/s)
2. Example Problems
(1) An object at rest on a horizontal surface experiences a 10 N horizontal pulling force for 3 s. The impulse of this pulling force on the object is ______ N·s.
Answer: 30
Detailed Explanation: Pulling force F = 10 N, action time t = 3 s, impulse I = Ft = 10 × 3 = 30 N·s.
(2) Two balls with equal mass fall freely from different heights. Given h₁:h₂ = 1:4, the ratio of their momenta at the instant they hit the ground is ______.
Answer: 1:2
Detailed Explanation: Two balls have equal mass, so m₁ = m₂. Falling freely from different heights, they undergo free fall motion: v² = 2gh, impact velocity v = √(2gh). Given h₁:h₂ = 1:4, the ratio of impact velocities:
v₁/v₂ = √(2gh₁)/√(2gh₂) = √(h₁/h₂) = √(1/4) = 1/2
So momentum ratio: p₁/p₂ = m₁v₁/m₂v₂ = v₁/v₂ = 1/2.

Section 2: Impulse-Momentum Theorem
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Impulse-Momentum Theorem: The change in an object's momentum over a time interval equals the impulse of the net force acting on it during that time.
Formula: I = p' - p or Ft = m(v' - v)
Where: I = impulse (N·s), p = initial momentum (kg·m/s), p' = final momentum (kg·m/s), F = force (N), t = time (s), m = mass (kg), v = initial velocity (m/s), v' = final velocity (m/s)
2. Example Problem
(1) A steel ball with mass m = 2 kg moves leftward at v₁ = 3 m/s, hits a hard wall and bounces back rightward at v₂ = 1 m/s. The impulse magnitude on the steel ball is ______ kg·m/s.
Answer: 8
Detailed Explanation: Momentum is a vector, its direction determined by velocity. Taking leftward as positive: initial momentum p = mv₁ = 2 × 3 = 6 kg·m/s; after hitting wall, moving rightward: final momentum p' = mv₂ = 2 × (-1) = -2 kg·m/s. By impulse-momentum theorem: I = p' - p = -2 - 6 = -8 kg·m/s. The negative sign relates to direction, so impulse magnitude is 8 kg·m/s.

Section 3: Conservation of Momentum
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Conservation of Momentum: When a system experiences no external forces or the vector sum of external forces is zero, the total momentum of the system remains constant.
Formula: p = p' (F_net = 0)
For two objects in a system: p₁ + p₂ = p₁' + p₂' or m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'

Chapter 5: Mechanical Energy
I. Basic Knowledge
Section 1: Work and Power
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Work: If an object is acted upon by a force and undergoes displacement in the direction of the force, work is done by the force. (Angle between force and displacement is denoted by α)
Formula: W = Fs cos α
Where: W = work, F = force acting on object, s = displacement of object
(2) Power: The ratio of work done to the time taken to do that work, denoted by P.
Formula: P = W/t = Fv
Where: P = power, W = work, t = time, F = force, v = velocity
2. Example Problems
(1) A 2 kg object on a smooth horizontal surface is pulled by a 4 N horizontal force through 6 m. Work done by pulling force F is ______ J, work done by gravity is ______ J.
Answer: 24; 0
Detailed Explanation: Pulling force magnitude 4 N, displacement 6 m, angle α = 0°, cos α = 1.
W = Fs cos α = 4 × 6 × 1 = 24 J.
Gravity perpendicular to displacement, α = 90°, cos α = 0, so W = 0.
(2) An external force F does 8 J of work on an object in 2 s. Power magnitude is ______ W.
Answer: 4
Detailed Explanation: Power P = W/t = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 W.

Section 4: Conservation of Mechanical Energy
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Conservation of Mechanical Energy: When only gravity and spring forces do work, kinetic and potential energy can convert into each other, total mechanical energy remains constant.
Formula: E₁ = E₂
E_k1 + E_p1 = E_k2 + E_p2
(2) Sum of kinetic and potential energy is mechanical energy.
Formula: E = E_p + E_k
2. Example Problem
A 1 kg small ball thrown horizontally at 10 m/s from 40 m height. Neglecting air resistance, find kinetic energy at impact.
Solution:
During fall, only gravity does work, satisfying mechanical energy conservation. Taking ground as reference:
Initial potential energy E_p1 = mgh = 1 × 10 × 40 = 400 J
Initial kinetic energy E_k1 = ½mv² = ½ × 1 × 10² = 50 J
At impact: potential energy E_p2 = 0 J, kinetic energy E_k2
By conservation: E_k1 + E_p1 = E_k2 + E_p2
400 + 50 = 0 + E_k2
E_k2 = 450 J
Answer: Impact kinetic energy 450 J.

Chapter 6: Electric Field
I. Basic Knowledge
Section 1: Charge and Coulomb's Law
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) There are only two types of charge in nature: positive charge and negative charge. Like charges repel each other; unlike charges attract each other.
(2) Law of Conservation of Charge: Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one object to another or from one part of an object to another. During transfer, the total charge remains constant.
(3) The amount of charge is called electric quantity (or simply charge). Its unit is the coulomb, abbreviated as C.
(4) Coulomb's Law: In a vacuum, the force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The direction of the force is along the line joining them.
Formula: F = k * (Q1 * Q2) / r^2
(5) When two identical metal spheres with charges Q1 and Q2 are brought into contact and then separated, the charge on each sphere becomes:
Formula: Q1' = Q2' = (Q1 + Q2) / 2
2. Example Problems
(1) A small sphere with charge Q1 = 5 × 10^(-4) C collides and makes full contact with another small sphere with charge Q2 = -3 × 10^(-4) C. After separating, the charge on each sphere is ______ C.
: 1 × 10^(-4)
[Explanation]: Q1' = Q2' = (Q1 + Q2) / 2 = (5 × 10^(-4) + (-3 × 10^(-4))) / 2 = 1 × 10^(-4) C.
(2) For two charges in a vacuum, if the distance between them remains constant and their charges are both increased to 4 times their original values, the Coulomb force between them increases to ______ times the original.
[Answer]: 16
[Explanation]: According to Coulomb's Law, F = k * (Q1 * Q2) / r^2.
Since the distance remains unchanged, r' = r.
The charges are both increased to 4 times: Q1' = 4Q1, Q2' = 4Q2.
Therefore, F' = k * (Q1' * Q2') / r'^2 = k * (4Q1 * 4Q2) / r^2 = 16 * [k * (Q1 * Q2) / r^2] = 16F.

Section 2: Electric Field and Electric Field Strength
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Electric Field Strength: The electric field strength at a point in an electric field is defined as the force F experienced by a test charge q placed at that point divided by the magnitude of the test charge q. It is a vector. Unit: N/C.
Formula: E = F_electric / q
The direction of the electric field strength is the same as the direction of the force on a positive test charge. The force on a negative test charge is opposite to the field direction.
(2) Electric Field Strength due to a Point Charge: The electric field strength at a distance r from a point charge Q is:
Formula: E = k * Q / r^2
Electric field lines of a positive point charge diverge; those of a negative point charge converge.
(3) The density of electric field lines indicates the strength of the electric field. Where lines are denser, the field is stronger; where lines are sparser, the field is weaker.
(4) Uniform Electric Field: In a region where the electric field strength is constant in both magnitude and direction, the field is uniform. Electric field lines in a uniform field are parallel and equally spaced.
2. Example Problems

: (3kq) / r^2
[Explanation]: Using the point charge field formula: E = k * Q / r^2. Here Q = 3q, so E = k * (3q) / r^2 = (3kq) / r^2.
(2) The electric field strength in a certain region is 5000 V/m. A point charge with a charge of 4 × 10^(-6) C placed in this field experiences an electric force of ______ N.
: 2 × 10^(-2)
[Explanation]: Using E = F / q, we have F = E * q = 5000 * (4 × 10^(-6)) = 2 × 10^(-2) N.

Section 3: Conductors in Electric Fields and Electrostatic Equilibrium*
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Electrostatic Equilibrium: A state where there is no net motion of charge within a conductor (including its surface). In electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside the conductor is zero everywhere.
(2) Electrostatic Shielding: A hollow conductor (a conducting shell) in electrostatic equilibrium has zero electric field inside its cavity. The interior region is thus unaffected by external electric fields. This phenomenon is called electrostatic shielding.
Section 4: Potential Difference and Electric Potential
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Potential Difference (Voltage): The work W_ab done by the electric force when a charge q moves from point a to point b, divided by the charge q, is called the potential difference between a and b.
Formula 1: U_ab = W_ab / q
Formula 2: U_ab = φ_a - φ_b (where φ_a is the electric potential at a, φ_b is at b)
U_ab: potential difference between a and b
W_ab: work done by the electric force
q: charge
(2) Equipotential Surface: A surface on which all points have the same electric potential.
(3) The electric potential decreases along the direction of electric field lines.
(4) In a uniform electric field, the potential difference between two points along the field direction equals the product of the electric field strength E and the distance d between the points along the field.
Formula: U = E * d
(5) When the electric force does positive work, W_ab > 0. When it does negative work (or work is done against the electric force), W_ab < 0.

I. Basic Knowledge
Section 1: Circuits and Ohm's Law
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Current: The current (I) is the charge (q) passing through a cross-section of a conductor divided by the time (t) taken.
Formula: I = q / t
Unit: Ampere (A)
(2) Ohm's Law: The current (I) through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage (U) across it and inversely proportional to its resistance (R).
Formula: I = U / R
(3) Resistance Law: The resistance (R) of a conductor is directly proportional to its length (l) and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area (S).
Formula: R = ρ * (l / S)
ρ is the resistivity.
(4) Electrical Work: The work done by the electric field when charge moves.
Formula: W = U * I * t
Unit: Joule (J)
(5) Heat (Joule's Law): The heat (Q) produced in a resistor equals the electrical work done on it.
Formula: Q = W = U * I * t
(6) Electrical Power: The rate of doing electrical work.
Formulas: P = W / t = U * I = I² * R = U² / R
Unit: Watt (W)
2. Example Problems
(1) A conductor carries a current I = 0.5 A. The charge that passes through it in 40 seconds is ______ C.
: 20
[Explanation]: q = I * t = 0.5 * 40 = 20 C.
(2) A lit light bulb has a voltage of U = 36 V and a current of I = 3 A. Its resistance is ______ Ω.
: 12
[Explanation]: R = U / I = 36 / 3 = 12 Ω.
*(3) A resistor wire originally 2 m long is stretched to twice its original length. Its resistance becomes ______ times the original.
: 2
[Explanation]: Resistance R is proportional to length l. If length doubles, resistance doubles.
(4) An electric motor has a rated voltage of 220 V and normally draws a current of 10 A. The work done by the motor in 1 minute is ______.
: 132,000 J
[Explanation]: W = U * I * t = 220 * 10 * 60 = 132,000 J.
(5) An electric motor has a rated voltage of 220 V and an operating resistance of 11 Ω. The motor's power is ______ W.
: 4400
[Explanation]: P = U² / R = (220)² / 11 = 4400 W.

1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Magnetic Flux Density (Magnetic Induction, B): In a magnetic field, the magnetic force F on a straight wire of length L carrying a current I, when the wire is perpendicular to the field, is proportional to I and L. The ratio F/(I L) is defined as the magnetic flux density B at that point.
Formula: B = F / (I L) (when B ⟂ I)
Unit: Tesla (T), 1 T = 1 N/(A·m)
Force on a Current-carrying Conductor (Ampere Force):
Formula: F = B I L (when B, I, and L are mutually perpendicular)
(2) Left-hand Rule: Stretch out the left hand so that the thumb is perpendicular to the other four fingers and all are in the same plane. Place the hand in the magnetic field with the magnetic field lines entering the palm. Point the four fingers in the direction of the conventional current. The direction the thumb points is the direction of the force on the conductor.

Chapter 9: Electromagnetic Induction
I. Basic Knowledge
Section 1: Law of Electromagnetic Induction
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Magnetic Flux (Φ): The product of the magnetic field component perpendicular to an area and the area itself. Φ = B·S (when B is perpendicular to the surface S). Unit: Weber (Wb); 1 Wb = 1 T·m².
(2) Electromagnetic Induction Phenomenon: When the magnetic flux through a closed loop changes, an electromotive force (emf) and thus an induced current are generated in the loop.
(3) Induced Electromotive Force (emf):
Faraday's Law of Induction: The magnitude of the induced emf in a circuit is equal to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the circuit. For a coil with N turns: E = N * |ΔΦ/Δt|.
Motional emf (conductor cutting magnetic field lines): When a straight conductor of length l moves with velocity v perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field B, the induced emf is E = B l v. If the motion is not perpendicular (angle θ between v and B), then E = B l v sinθ.

Chapter 10: Mechanical Vibrations and Waves
I. Basic Knowledge
Section 1: Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): A type of periodic motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement from equilibrium and acts in the opposite direction.
Formula: F = -k * x
k: spring constant (stiffness)
x: displacement from equilibrium
(2) Characteristics of SHM:
Period (T): Time for one complete cycle. Unit: second (s)
Frequency (f): Number of cycles per second. f = 1 / T. Unit: Hertz (Hz)
Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement from equilibrium.
(3) Equations for SHM:
Displacement: x = A * cos(ωt + φ) or x = A * sin(ωt + φ)
Angular frequency: ω = 2πf = 2π / T
Velocity: v = -Aω * sin(ωt + φ)
Acceleration: a = -Aω² * cos(ωt + φ) = -ω² * x
2. Example Problems
(1) A mass on a spring oscillates with a period T = 2 s. Its frequency is ______ Hz.
[Answer]: 0.5
[Explanation]: f = 1 / T = 1 / 2 = 0.5 Hz.
(2) The displacement of an object in SHM is given by x = 0.1 * cos(2πt) meters. Its amplitude is ______ m and its period is ______ s.
[Answer]: 0.1; 1
[Explanation]: Comparing with x = A cos(ωt): A = 0.1 m. ω = 2π rad/s, so T = 2π / ω = 1 s.

Chapter 11: Heat

1. Basic Knowledge

Section 2: Specific Heat and Latent Heat
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Specific Heat Capacity (c): The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K).
Formula: Q = m * c * ΔT
Q: heat added/removed (J)
m: mass (kg)
c: specific heat (J/(kg·°C))
ΔT: temperature change (°C or K)
(2) Latent Heat: Heat absorbed or released during a phase change (melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation) at constant temperature.
Latent heat of fusion (L_f): for solid-liquid change.
Latent heat of vaporization (L_v): for liquid-gas change.
Formula: Q = m * L
2. Example Problems

(2) The latent heat of fusion for ice is 334,000 J/kg. Heat required to melt 0.5 kg of ice at 0°C is ______ J.

Chapter 12: Geometrical Optics
I. Basic Knowledge
Section 1: Reflection of Light
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Laws of Reflection:
The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the surface all lie in the same plane.
The angle of incidence (i) equals the angle of reflection (r). (i = r)
(2) Image Formation by Plane Mirrors:
The image is virtual, upright, and the same size as the object.
The image is located as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
2. Example Problem
A light ray strikes a plane mirror at an angle of incidence of 30°. The angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray is ______°.
[Answer]: 60
[Explanation]: i = r = 30°. The angle between incident and reflected rays is i + r = 60°.

Section 2: Refraction of Light
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Refraction: The bending of light when it passes from one medium into another with a different optical density.
(2) Snell's Law (Law of Refraction): n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂.
n₁, n₂ are the refractive indices of medium 1 and 2.
θ₁ is the angle of incidence (in medium 1).
θ₂ is the angle of refraction (in medium 2).
(3) Refractive Index (n): n = c / v, where c is the speed of light in vacuum (≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s) and v is the speed of light in the medium. n ≥ 1.
(4) Total Internal Reflection: Occurs when light travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium (n₁ > n₂) and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle θ_c. sinθ_c = n₂ / n₁.
2. Example Problem
Light travels from air (n₁ ≈ 1) into water (n₂ = 1.33). If the angle of incidence is 30°, the angle of refraction is approximately ______°.
[Answer]: 22
[Explanation]: Using Snell's Law: 1 * sin30° = 1.33 * sinθ₂. sinθ₂ = 0.5 / 1.33 ≈ 0.376. θ₂ ≈ arcsin(0.376) ≈ 22°.

Section 3: Lenses
1. Basic Knowledge
(1) Convex Lens (Converging Lens):
Can form real or virtual images.
Focal length (f) is positive.
Thin lens formula: 1/f = 1/u + 1/v, where u = object distance, v = image distance.
Magnification: M = h_i / h_o = -v / u.
(2) Concave Lens (Diverging Lens):
Always forms virtual, upright, and diminished images.
Focal length (f) is negative.
2. Example Problem
